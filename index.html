Chatbase in 2026: Pricing, Limits and What It Actually Does

Chatbase sells one agent that answers across chat, email and voice. This is an outside read on where that holds up and where it does not.

Who this is forSupport teams drowning in repetitive tickets are the obvious buyer, and the enterprise logos on the homepage suggest the platform survives contact with large, cautious organisations. Small teams without documented answers will get less out of it, because an agent trained on a thin knowledge base produces confident nonsense and a support queue full of escalations. The caveat nobody puts on a pricing page is that deployment is mostly content work, not configuration. And if the thing actually blocking your launch is the site the agent is supposed to sit on, that is a different problem, solved by something like Begin.sh rather than by a chatbot vendor.

The positioning, in the vendor's own words

The homepage calls it conversational agents for customer experience: agents that meet customers at every stage of their journey across chat, email and voice, resolve issues end to end and increase revenue. That last clause is the tell. This is no longer sold as a widget that deflects questions, it is sold as a revenue surface, which is why the product breaks into three named roles. A support agent resolves queries across live chat, email, phone, Slack and more. A sales agent engages prospects, answers product questions and steers conversations toward revenue, with an example on the page about whether a Pro plan suits a team. A third agent handles product guidance. One underlying system, three job descriptions, sold to three different budget holders.

Logo walls are cheap, but the composition of this one is informative. Dolby, Pearson, Roche, Bridgestone, National Grid, Sage, EF Education First, Jumia and Alpian are not startups experimenting with a side project; several sit in regulated or safety-conscious industries where procurement asks hard questions before an AI touches a customer. Alongside them are consumer brands such as F45 and Chuck E. Cheese, and the site links out to individual customer stories for several accounts. There is a dedicated enterprise page, a demo request path separate from the self-serve trial, and a rating displayed near the top of the page. None of that proves the product works for your use case, but it does suggest the security review is a path others have already walked.

The part that is your job, not the software's

Every agent platform inherits the quality of what you feed it. If your help centre is three years stale, your refund policy lives in a manager's head and your product names differ between the app and the marketing site, the agent will reproduce all of it at speed and with unearned confidence. Budget for a content pass before the rollout: write down the twenty questions that make up most of your ticket volume, answer each one canonically, and decide explicitly what the agent must never attempt. Then plan an escalation path a human can trust. Teams that treat this as a two-week configuration project tend to discover the real work in week three, usually while a customer is reading the output.

Pricing and the questions to ask on the demo

There is a pricing page and a free trial, plus a separate enterprise track with a demo request, which almost always means the published tiers stop well before the features an enterprise buyer wants. The homepage does not print rates, so ask directly on the call: how usage is metered, whether voice and email cost the same as chat, what happens when you pass the plan's ceiling mid-month, and which integrations sit behind the enterprise tier. Ask for the resolution measurement definition too, since deflection, resolution and containment are counted differently by almost every vendor and the difference decides whether your reported numbers mean anything six months in.

Running a trial that tells you something

  1. Pull your twenty real questions
    Export last month's tickets and take the twenty most repeated. These are your test set. Anything the agent cannot answer here will not be rescued by a bigger plan.
  2. Fix the source content first
    Write one canonical answer per question, including the awkward ones about refunds and edge cases. The agent can only be as consistent as the material behind it.
  3. Define the handoff rule
    Decide which topics always go to a human, and check that the escalation actually carries the conversation across. A clean handoff matters more to customers than a clever answer.
  4. Measure against a baseline
    Record current first-response time and ticket volume before launch. Without that number, any improvement the dashboard reports afterwards is unfalsifiable.

FAQ

What does Chatbase do?

It provides conversational agents for customer experience, described on its own site as meeting customers across chat, email and voice to resolve issues end to end. The platform splits into a support agent, a sales agent and a product guidance agent, sharing one system but aimed at different teams and outcomes.

How much does it cost?

The site has a pricing page and offers a free trial, and the homepage quotes no rates. Enterprise buyers go through a separate demo path, which usually means custom pricing. Check the official pricing page for current numbers rather than any figure quoted second-hand, including here.

Which channels are supported?

The homepage names live chat, email and voice at the top, and the support agent description adds phone and Slack with an open-ended reference to more. Confirm the specific channel you depend on during a trial, since channel coverage and plan tier are often linked.

Is it only for large companies?

No. There is a self-serve free trial alongside the enterprise track, so small teams can start without a sales call. The practical limit is content rather than company size: a business without documented answers will struggle regardless of which vendor it picks.

How long does a rollout take?

The software side is quick; the content side is not. Most of the effort goes into writing canonical answers, setting escalation rules and testing against real tickets. Teams that plan for a configuration exercise usually find themselves doing a documentation project instead.
